Historical research and illustrated methods

The degree–diameter problem asks how many vertices a simple undirected graph can have when each vertex has at most Δ neighbors and every pair of vertices is connected by a path of length at most D. Earlier ARIS research developed neighborhood extensions, finite-field layers and geometric connection reallocation. The earlier (10,4) result was obtained using MathRSI v2 and its degree–diameter plugin, continuing that research with its saved constructions and failure trajectories. Execution version.

How the constructions work

1. Extend a graph through covering neighborhoods

Keep the original edges and connect a new vertex x to a set S of existing vertices. If the radius-(D−1) neighborhoods around S cover the current graph, every vertex is within D steps of x. Each selected neighbor must also have room for the new edge. Repeating this argument gives the D5 extension sequences; neighborhood cloning supports related D3 and D4 constructions.

Connecting x to opposite corners 0 and 7 covers all eight original vertices within three steps, with degrees at most 4.

The interactive illustration compares four choices on a cube: one attachment misses a vertex; complementary attachments cover it; reusing the same attachment points exceeds their degree limit; choosing different points allows another extension.

The earlier (10,4) construction extends this idea to a graph with no unused degree capacity. Starting from 2,566 vertices, we remove five disjoint edges and connect a new vertex to all ten endpoints. Every affected old vertex returns to degree 10, and the new vertex has degree 10. Failed point pairs guided the subsequent choices of edges; the final 2,567-vertex graph passes the complete distance check.

Exact recipe, construction program and verification accompany the ten accepted intermediate versions from 2,558 to 2,567 vertices.

2. Lift a small controller over a finite field

The 3,840-vertex, degree-20 graph uses 60 controller states, each carrying the 64 points of F₄³. Five controller ports supply four neighbors each. Compatible affine maps make three-step routes cover the required coordinate layers. Moving from shared parameters to edge-specific maps resolved the compatibility obstacle in the F4 construction.

3. Reallocate geometric connections

Spreads and ovoids in generalized quadrangles organize compatible filling and cloning positions. In the 7,531 → 7,532 construction, 17 clone–filler edges are replaced by 17 edges from a new hub to the fillers. Alternative short routes preserve the distance bound, while the edge count remains 97,750.

The construction builds on source graphs and controllers by Mizuno, Rajiv, Yugeswardeenoo, and Bhakar/f4t4nt, together with Delorme's and subsequent finite-geometry methods. Construction notes connect the illustrations to the underlying arguments and source programs.

What carries over to new problems

The successful steps changed how connections were chosen: combine neighborhoods that cover different regions, allocate degree capacity across all additions, and replace a connection only when the affected routes can be recovered. These lessons turn an individual construction into a starting point for a family of parameters. The proofs, counterexamples and construction programs informed MathRSI's degree–diameter plugin, which connects these methods to the research workflows in v2.
