# Unit repeat triangles when the repeat support is connected

## Claim and status

**PROVABLE AS STATED:** the intersection classification, separation,
transport identity, and strengthened packing bound below follow from the
actual graph identities and the cited D1 reductions. This is a new paper
derivation awaiting independent review.

The stronger claim that every unit triangle of $B$ is generated by a
double repeat is **not currently justified**. The cases with zero or one
common original neighbor remain open after the deductions below.

## Assumptions and notation

Let $G$ be an actual 62-vertex graph of maximum degree eight and diameter
at most two, with adjacency matrix $A$ and actual repeat matrix

$$B=A^2+A-7I-J.$$

Assume explicitly that the positive support of $B$ is connected. This
note does not prove that this connectedness assumption is automatic.
Use the established reductions: $A$ is eight-regular; $B$ is symmetric,
nonnegative integral, zero-diagonal, of row sum three; $AB=BA$; and all
entries of $B$ are at most two. For distinct vertices,

$$|N_G(v)\cap N_G(w)|+A_{vw}=1+B_{vw}. \tag{1}$$

Triangle pairing would give a closed six-vertex repeat component for an
original triangle. A D0 double repeat would give a closed four-vertex
repeat component. Connectedness therefore implies that $G$ is
triangle-free and every double repeat is D1. Write $d$ for the number
of double repeats, $D$ for their $2d$ endpoints, and $\mathcal S$ for
the union of their generated triangles.

Use `GLOBAL_DOUBLE_TRIANGLE_QUOTIENT.md` for the disjoint generated
triangles and the original port count; use
`D1_MATCH_HARD_BRANCH_REDUCTION.md` for the distinct external ports of a
D1 generated triangle, and `D1_MATCH_C4_TRANSPORT_REFINEMENT.md` for the
fact that a D1 heavy endpoint's unit mate belongs to no unit $B$ triangle
and is not a heavy endpoint.

A **unit triangle** is a three-set $S=\{x,y,z\}$ with all three
off-diagonal repeat entries equal to one. Let

$$t(S)=|N_G(x)\cap N_G(y)\cap N_G(z)|.$$

Because $G$ is triangle-free, an original edge has repeat entry zero.
Thus $S$ is independent in $G$, and each pair in $S$ has exactly two
common original neighbors by (1).

## Proof strategy and dependency map

1. The exact pairwise common-neighbor counts classify $t(S)$ and its
   witness sets.
2. Saturated rows and the three distinct ports of a D1 generated
   triangle separate generated triangles from every other unit triangle.
3. Row budgets and commutation give exact consequences for the remaining
   two types, including a uniform neighborhood transport identity.
4. The D1 light-mate restriction gives a stronger count after removing
   vertices on all unit triangles, not only the generated ones.

## 1. Complete intersection classification

The only possibilities are

$$t(S)\in\{0,1,2\}. \tag{2}$$

If $t(S)=2$, let the two common neighbors be $u,v$. They are
nonadjacent because they share a neighbor in a triangle-free graph.
They have at least the three common neighbors $x,y,z$, so (1) and the
bound $B_{uv}\le2$ force

$$B_{uv}=2,\qquad N_G(u)\cap N_G(v)=S.$$

Thus $S$ is exactly the generated triangle of the double repeat
$\{u,v\}$. Conversely a generated triangle has these two common
neighbors, and cannot have a third because each pair in the triangle
already has its exact two common neighbors. Therefore

$$S\text{ is generated}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t(S)=2. \tag{3}$$

If $t(S)=1$, write $u$ for the common neighbor. The pairwise intersections
have the unique form

$$\begin{aligned}
N_G(x)\cap N_G(y)&=\{u,a\},\\
N_G(y)\cap N_G(z)&=\{u,b\},\\
N_G(z)\cap N_G(x)&=\{u,c\},
\end{aligned} \tag{4}$$

where $u,a,b,c$ are distinct. The vertices $a,b,c$ have respectively
the neighborhoods $\{x,y\},\{y,z\},\{z,x\}$ within $S$; any additional
adjacency to $S$ would give a second common neighbor of all three.

If $t(S)=0$, the pairwise intersections are six distinct vertices,

$$\begin{aligned}
N_G(x)\cap N_G(y)&=\{a_1,a_2\},\\
N_G(y)\cap N_G(z)&=\{b_1,b_2\},\\
N_G(z)\cap N_G(x)&=\{c_1,c_2\}.
\end{aligned} \tag{5}$$

An equality between witnesses for different pairs would produce a
common neighbor of all three, so these six vertices are distinct.

These are the complete intersection patterns of the three neighbor
sets; they do not prescribe all edges elsewhere in the graph.

## 2. Generated triangles meet no other unit triangle

Two different unit triangles cannot meet at just one vertex: that
vertex would require four distinct positive repeat neighbors, beyond
its row sum three. If they share an edge, write them as
$\{x,y,z\}$ and $\{x,y,w\}$. The row at $x$ is then exactly
$e_y+e_z+e_w$, and the row at $y$ exactly $e_x+e_z+e_w$.
Relative to the first triangle, the external repeat ports of $x$ and
$y$ are the same vertex $w$.

For a D1 generated triangle the established complete rows have three
pairwise distinct external ports, denoted $4,b,c$ in the normalized
D1 proof. Hence it cannot share an edge with another unit triangle.
It follows that

$$\boxed{\text{Every generated triangle is vertex-disjoint from every
different unit triangle.}} \tag{6}$$

In particular all triangles of type zero or one are disjoint from
$\mathcal S$. No vertex of any unit triangle is a double endpoint,
since two unit triangle entries already consume two units of its
repeat row and leave only one.

## 3. Exact rows and restrictions for type one

Use (4). Each pair among $u,a,b,c$ shares an original neighbor in
$S$, so these four vertices form an independent set in $G$.
The pairs $(u,a),(u,b),(u,c)$ each have at least two common original
neighbors. Consequently their repeat entries are at least one, and
the row sum of $u$ forces the complete row

$$\boxed{B_u=e_a+e_b+e_c.} \tag{7}$$

Every unit-triangle vertex has exactly one external unit port. Write

$$B_x=e_y+e_z+e_{p_x},\quad
  B_y=e_x+e_z+e_{p_y},\quad
  B_z=e_x+e_y+e_{p_z}, \tag{8}$$

where the ports lie outside $S$ but are not assumed distinct.
Using (7), the $(x,u)$ entry of commutation is

$$2=A_{xa}+A_{xb}+A_{xc}
   =(AB)_{xu}=(BA)_{xu}=2+A_{p_xu}.$$

Thus $A_{p_xu}=0$. The $(y,u)$ and $(z,u)$ entries give

$$\boxed{u\text{ is adjacent to none of }p_x,p_y,p_z.} \tag{9}$$

The center $u$ itself cannot lie in a generated triangle. Indeed any
unit triangle containing $u$ must use two vertices among $a,b,c$ by
(7). The exact intersections

$$N_G(u)\cap N_G(a)=\{x,y\},\quad
N_G(u)\cap N_G(b)=\{y,z\},\quad
N_G(u)\cap N_G(c)=\{z,x\}$$

show that any such triangle has exactly one common original neighbor.
For instance a triangle $\{u,a,b\}$ has exactly the common neighbor
$y$. It is therefore type one by (3). This proves

$$u\notin D\cup\mathcal S. \tag{10}$$

Here exclusion from $D$ follows from its three unit entries (7).
The center may still lie on another type-one triangle; the argument
does not exclude that case.

There is nevertheless only one unit triangle for which $u$ is a
common original neighbor. To see this, let $v,w\in N_G(u)$ have a
positive repeat. They are nonadjacent, and a second common neighbor
$r\ne u$ of $v,w$ must have $B_{ur}\ge1$. By (7), $r$ is one of
$a,b,c$. Its exact two neighbors within $N_G(u)$ are, respectively,
$\{x,y\},\{y,z\},\{z,x\}$. Therefore

$$B[N_G(u)]\text{ consists exactly of the unit triangle }S
  \text{ and five isolated vertices}. \tag{10a}$$

In particular the assignment of a type-one triangle to its unique
common center is injective. This does not mean that the centers lie
outside all unit triangles; (10) only excludes generated triangles.

## 4. Exact restrictions for type zero

Use the six witnesses in (5). Any two of these witnesses share at
least one original neighbor in $S$, so the six-set is independent in
$G$. Its repeat matrix has no double entry: a double between two of
its vertices would generate a triangle containing their shared
neighbor in $S$. This generated triangle would meet $S$, contrary to
(6), since $S$ is not generated.

Within each of the three displayed witness pairs, there are already
two common original neighbors. The absence of a double therefore
gives the exact entries and common-neighbor sets

$$\begin{aligned}
B_{a_1a_2}=1,&\qquad N_G(a_1)\cap N_G(a_2)=\{x,y\},\\
B_{b_1b_2}=1,&\qquad N_G(b_1)\cap N_G(b_2)=\{y,z\},\\
B_{c_1c_2}=1,&\qquad N_G(c_1)\cap N_G(c_2)=\{z,x\}.
\end{aligned} \tag{11}$$

Thus the induced repeat graph on the six witnesses is simple and
contains these three matching edges. Additional unit entries between
the three pairs have not been excluded. Neither (11) nor the current
D1 lemmas force a contradiction.

## 5. Exact neighborhood counts and a uniform transport identity

For any unit triangle $S$, put

$$q(v)=|N_G(v)\cap S|,\qquad
  n_i=|\{v:q(v)=i\}|\quad(0\le i\le3).$$

Counting degrees and pairwise common neighbors gives

$$n_3=t(S),\qquad n_2+3n_3=6,\qquad
  n_1+2n_2+3n_3=24.$$

Since the whole graph has 62 vertices, these identities give the
complete four-stratum counts

$$\boxed{(n_0,n_1,n_2,n_3)
  =(44-t,\ 12+3t,\ 6-3t,\ t).} \tag{12}$$

The zero stratum includes $S$ itself. Let $h$ be the external-port
multiplicity vector, so $h(v)$ is the number of ports in (8) equal
to $v$. Its support lies outside $S$ and $\sum_v h(v)=3$. Then

$$B\mathbf1_S=2\mathbf1_S+h,\qquad
  A\mathbf1_S=q.$$

Applying $AB=BA$ to $\mathbf1_S$ yields the exact nonnegative transport
identity

$$\boxed{(B-2I)q=Ah.} \tag{13}$$

No distinction between repeated and distinct ports is suppressed in
(13). It is a necessary identity for all three intersection types,
not an assertion that these data can be extended to an actual graph.

## 6. Packing all unit triangles strengthens the port bound

Let $U$ be the set of vertices lying on any unit $B$ triangle, and let
$v_{\mathrm{extra}}=|U\setminus\mathcal S|$. By (6) and the disjointness
of generated triangles,

$$|U|=3d+v_{\mathrm{extra}},\qquad D\cap U=\varnothing.$$

Every one of the $2d$ heavy endpoints has exactly one unit mate.
The D1 light-mate theorem places every such mate outside both $D$
and $U$. A vertex has repeat-row capacity three, so counting these
unit incidences gives

$$2d\le3\bigl(62-2d-|U|\bigr).$$

Equivalently,

$$\boxed{17d+3v_{\mathrm{extra}}\le186.} \tag{14}$$

This improves the earlier connected-case bound $17d\le186$ when
nongenerated unit triangles exist. With the established oddness of
$d$, it gives $d\in\{1,3,5,7,9\}$. If $d=9$, it additionally gives
$v_{\mathrm{extra}}\le11$.

The overlap structure of unit triangles has a precise elementary
form. In a graph of maximum degree three, triangles can intersect
only by sharing an edge. Two sharing an edge form a diamond
($K_4$ with one edge removed). A third triangle meeting this diamond
would complete the $K_4$: the two shared-edge endpoints already
have all three neighbors, leaving no other way to add such a
triangle. A unit $K_4$ would exhaust four repeat rows and form a
closed component, contradicting connectedness on 62 vertices.
Consequently every connected cluster of mutually overlapping unit
triangles is a single triangle or a diamond. Here adjacency of
clusters means vertex overlap, not arbitrary repeat edges between
them.

Generated triangles are single-triangle clusters by (6). If the
nongenerated clusters consist of $s$ single triangles and $k$
diamonds, then

$$v_{\mathrm{extra}}=3s+4k,\qquad
  \boxed{17d+9s+12k\le186.} \tag{15}$$

Neither count asserts that all such integer choices are realizable.

## Remaining obstacle

Types zero and one have not been excluded. Type one forces the
three-unit star row (7) at its common center, but does not force that
center to be a heavy endpoint or a light mate. Type zero forces the
simple matching (11) among its six witnesses, but its other repeat
neighbors and original neighbors are still undetermined. The exact
transport equation (13) remains compatible with these incomplete
row descriptions at the level proved here. No actual 62-vertex graph
realizing either pattern has been constructed, and no local partial
pattern is claimed to certify global realizability.

The next missing result would need to constrain these additional rows
or their interaction with actual D1 heavy edges. Replacing this missing
result by the assumption that every unit triangle is generated would
be an unjustified strengthening of the established theory.
