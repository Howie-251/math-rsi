Abstract
Quotients of incidence graphs of generalized polygons by polarities are a classical source
of large graphs of given maximum degree and diameter. The construction needs less than
a polarity: if a connected bipartite graph has diameter n, its quotient by any group of
automorphisms containing an element that interchanges the two parts has diameter at most
n − 1. The split Cayley hexagon H(9) has no polarity, but it has dualities of order four.
We show that any two of them generate conjugate subgroups of the automorphism group
of its incidence graph, and that the quotient by such a duality has 33397 vertices, of which
33033 have degree 10, 336 have degree 7 and 28 have degree 6, and diameter 5. Adding edges
and twin vertices we obtain N (10, 5) ≥ 33397, N (11, 5) ≥ 35203 and N (12, 5) ≥ 38167 for
the degree-diameter problem; the previously published lower bounds were 14047, 20952 and
34992. For the other symplectic quadrangles W (2h) and split Cayley hexagons H(3h) whose
incidence graphs have degree at most 20, the quotient by a duality of minimal order does
not improve the known bounds.

Many of the constructions that come closest to the Moore bound are geometric. Let S be
a generalized n-gon of order ( q, q). Its incidence graph Γ is ( q + 1)-regular and bipartite, with
diameter n and girth 2n. A polarity of S is an automorphism of Γ of order two that interchanges
points and lines, and the quotient of Γ by a polarity has maximum degreeq +1, diameter at most
n − 1 and half as many vertices as Γ. For projective planes these are the polarity graphs of Erd˝ os
and R´ enyi [11] and of Brown [3], of diameter two. Delorme [8, 9] used polarities of generalized
quadrangles and hexagons; the quotients of W (8) and H(3) are the largest known graphs of
degree 9 and diameter 3 and of degree 4 and diameter 5. Polarity quotients also underlie the
PolarFly network topology [18] and recent constructions of Isham, Lakhotia, Monroe and Petrini
[16].
A self-dual generalized polygon need not have a polarity [17, 2]. The symplectic quadrangle
W (q) is self-dual if and only if q is even, and has a polarity if and only if q is an odd power of
2; the split Cayley hexagon H(q) is self-dual if and only if q is a power of 3, and has a polarity
if and only if q is an odd power of 3 [27, 28, 29]. The argument for polarity graphs, however,
does not use that the polarity is an involution. It uses only an automorphism that interchanges
the two parts of Γ (Lemma 3.1), so every duality gives a quotient of diameter at most n − 1.
A duality of order 2 m gives a quotient with roughly |V (Γ)|/2m vertices, so for m > 1 the
quotient can compete only where the known bounds are well below the Moore bound. Among
the quadrangles and hexagons of Section 6 this happens only for H(9), at diameter 5.
Theorem 1.1. Let Γ be the incidence graph of the split Cayley hexagon H(9).
(i) H(9) has dualities of order four and no polarity, and any two dualities of order four generate
conjugate subgroups of Aut(Γ).
(ii) If τ is a duality of order four, then F = Γ/⟨τ ⟩ has 33397 vertices and 166425 edges; 33033
of its vertices have degree 10, 336 have degree 7 and 28 have degree 6; and F has diameter
5.

2.1 Quotient graphs
All graphs are finite and simple. For a graph Γ and a vertex x we write N (x) for the set of
neighbours of x, and Fix(g) for the set of vertices fixed by an automorphism g. Let G ≤ Aut(Γ).

2.2 Generalized polygons
Let n ≥ 3. A generalized n-gon is a point-line incidence structure whose incidence graph, the
bipartite graph on points and lines with edges given by incidence, has diameter n and girth 2n.
It has order (s, t) if every line has s + 1 points and every point lies on t + 1 lines, and it is thick if
s, t≥ 2. By the theorem of Feit and Higman [12], a thick generalized n-gon has n ∈ {3, 4, 6, 8},
and a thick generalized octagon does not have s = t. The incidence graph of a generalized n-gon
of order ( q, q) is ( q + 1)-regular with
|V (Γ)| = 2 (1 +q + q2 + · · ·+ qn−1) (2.3)
vertices, which is the largest order of a bipartite graph of maximum degree q + 1 and diameter
n [25, 21].
Since the incidence graph is connected and bipartite, an automorphism either preserves the
set of points or interchanges points and lines; it is a collineation in the first case and a duality in
the second. The collineations form a subgroup of index at most two. The structure is self-dual
if it has a duality, and a polarity is a duality of order two. A point x is absolute for a polarity
π if it is incident with the line πx.

3 Quotients by groups containing a duality
Lemma 3.1. Let Γ be a connected bipartite graph of diameter n, and let G ≤ Aut(Γ) contain
an element that interchanges the two parts. Then Γ/G has maximum degree at most that of Γ
and diameter at most n − 1.
Proof. The degree bound is (2.1). Let π ∈ G interchange the parts, and let x, ybe vertices.
Because Γ is bipartite, dΓ(x, y) is even if x and y lie in the same part and odd otherwise, and
dΓ(x, πy) has the other parity. Both distances are at most n and they are not equal, so one of
them is at most n − 1. By (2.2), dΓ/G(¯x, ¯y) ≤ n − 1.
When G is generated by a polarity this is the classical argument for polarity graphs; see [9]
and [16, Prop. 4.10]. We have not found the case of a larger group in the literature, although
the proof is the same.

4 The quotient of H(9)
For an integer h let v2(h) denote the exponent of the largest power of two dividing h.
Proposition 4.1. Let S be H(q) with q = 3 h, or W (q) with q = 2 h. Every duality of S has
order divisible by 2v2(h)+1. In particular S has a polarity if and only if h is odd.
Proof. By (F1), Aut(Γ)/G is cyclic of order 2h, generated by the image of δ, and a duality maps
to δiG with i odd. This element has order 2 h/ gcd(i, 2h). Since gcd( i, 2h) is odd, the order is
divisible by 2v2(h)+1, and the order of a duality is divisible by the order of its image. Conversely,
if h is odd then δh is a duality of order two.
For q = 9 and q = 4 we have h = 2, so every duality has order divisible by four, and δ has
order four. The next result shows that when h is a power of two, every duality of the smallest
possible order generates a conjugate of ⟨δ⟩.

that is, a polarity π of H(3). The 728 vertices of Γ 3 therefore form 364 orbits of size two, each

We compute degrees with (2.1). Let ¯x be an orbit of size four, and suppose that two distinct
neighbours y, y′ of x lie in the same orbit. They have the same type, so y′ = τ 2y. Applying τ 2
to the edges xy and xy′ shows that τ 2x is adjacent to y′ and to y. As τ 2x ̸= x, the vertices
x, y, τ2x, y′ form a cycle of length four, which contradicts the fact that Γ has girth 12. If a
neighbour of x lay in ¯x, it would be τ xor τ 3x, and applying powers of τ would show that
x, τ x, τ2x, τ3x form a cycle of length four, again a contradiction. Hence every orbit of size four
has degree 10.
Now let x be a point of H(3), so that ¯x = {x, τ x}. Of the ten lines of H(9) through x, four
are lines of H(3). These are fixed by τ 2 and lie in four different orbits of size two. The other six
are permuted by the involution τ 2 without fixed points, and they lie in three orbits of size four,
each containing one pair {L, τ2L}. The only one of these seven orbits that can equal ¯ x is the
orbit of the line τ x, and this happens exactly when x is incident with τ x= πx, that is, when x
is an absolute point of π. So ¯x has degree 6 if x is absolute and degree 7 otherwise. A polarity
of H(3) has 3 3 + 1 = 28 absolute points [29], so 28 orbits have degree 6 and 336 have degree 7.
Counting edges, (33033 · 10 + 336 · 7 + 28 · 6)/2 = 166425.
By Lemma 3.1, F has diameter at most 5. It has more than M (10, 4) = 8201 vertices, so its
diameter is exactly 5.
5 Degrees 10, 11 and 12
5.1 A 10-regular graph
By Theorem 1.1, the 364 vertices of F of degree less than 10 lack 336 · 3 + 28 · 4 = 1120 edge
ends in total. We found a set of 560 edges, each joining two of these vertices, none of them an
edge of F , and meeting each such vertex v in exactly 10 − degF v edges. Adding them to F gives
a 10-regular graph F ∗ with 33397 vertices and 5 · 33397 edges. Adding edges does not increase
distances, so F ∗ has diameter 5.

By Remark 3.3, a quotient by a duality of order at least four can compete only if the incidence
graph is close to the bound (2.3), so we consider generalized polygons. A duality forces s = t,
which rules out thick octagons [12].

7 Computations and data
We constructed Γ from the coordinates of Section 2.3 and checked that it is 10-regular and
bipartite on 132860 vertices, and that breadth-first search from sampled vertices gives layers
of sizes 1 , 10, 90, 810, 7290, 65610, 59049, as in a generalized hexagon of order (9 , 9). To find a
duality we used the quotient Γ /⟨φ⟩, with its vertices coloured by orbit size. Its automorphism
group, computed with nauty [20], has order 8491392 = 2 |G2(3)| and contains an element of
order two that interchanges the two types of vertices. This element lifts to a duality τ of Γ
with τ 2 = φ, and Γ /⟨τ ⟩ has the vertex count and degrees given in Theorem 1.1. As a check
on Theorem 4.2, we formed dualities gτ with g a random product of collineations; each had
order divisible by four, and each one of order four gave a quotient with the same vertex count
and degrees. The completion F ∗ was found by a randomized greedy procedure, and the twin
assignments by a greedy procedure followed by local exchanges.
Every graph in Table 1 was checked by breadth-first search from every vertex, which confirms
the maximum degree and that every eccentricity is exactly 5. These graphs are not vertex-
transitive, so a search from a single vertex would not suffice.
