Abstract
Let Γ be the incidence graph of the split Cayley hexagon H(9) and τ a duality of order
four. The quotient F = Γ /⟨τ ⟩ is a graph on 33397 vertices of maximum degree 10 and
diameter 5; it identifies each point p of H(9) with the point τ 2p. We show how to undo
this identification on any set of such pairs while keeping the diameter at most 5. For every
bijection σ from the points to the lines of a generalized hexagon, the graph on the points in
which x and y are adjacent whenever one of them lies on the image of the other has diameter
at most 5. Choosing σ(p) between τ pand τ −1p according to a colouring of the pairs, the
partial fold that keeps a set S of pairs apart has 33397 + |S| vertices and diameter at most
5, and its degrees are determined by S and the colouring. Together with a blow-up of the
points of the subhexagon H(3) and twin vertices, integer programming over these choices
gives N (11, 5) ≥ 36563, N (12, 5) ≥ 41699 and N (13, 5) ≥ 50750 for the degree-diameter
problem; the previously published lower bounds were 35203, 38167 and 44232.
MSC 2020: 05C35 (primary); 05B25, 05C25, 51E12 (secondary).
Keywords: degree-diameter problem, generalized hexagon, duality, polarity graph, quotient

Quotients of incidence graphs of generalized polygons by polarities are a classical source
of such constructions: the polarity graphs of projective planes of Erd˝ os and R´ enyi [9] and of
Brown [2] have diameter two, and Delorme [6, 7] used polarities of generalized quadrangles and
hexagons. If Γ is the incidence graph of a generalized n-gon of order ( q, q) and π is a polarity,
the quotient Γ/⟨π⟩ has maximum degree q + 1 and diameter at most n − 1. In [15] we observed
that the argument works for any group of automorphisms that contains a duality, and applied
it to the split Cayley hexagon H(9), which has no polarity but has dualities of order four. If τ
is one of them, the quotient F = Γ/⟨τ ⟩ has 33397 vertices, maximum degree 10 and diameter
5 (Theorem 2.1 below). Together with twin vertices it gave the bounds N (10, 5) ≥ 33397,
N (11, 5) ≥ 35203 and N (12, 5) ≥ 38167. A companion paper [17] gives further constructions
from generalized quadrangles and hexagons, by other means.
The quotient F identifies every point p of H(9) with the point τ 2p. In this paper we undo this
identification on part of H(9) while keeping the diameter at most 5. The key is an observation
about pairings of points with lines in a generalized hexagon (Lemma 3.1): for every bijection
σ from points to lines, the graph on the points in which x and y are adjacent whenever one of
them lies on the image of the other has diameter at most 5. For a polarity σ this is the polarity
graph. We take σ(p) ∈ {τ p, τ−1p}, chosen by a colouring of the pairs {p, τ2p}, and show that
the resulting graph maps onto F , its edges between two neighbouring pairs forming a perfect
matching or a complete bipartite graph K2,2 (Lemma 4.1). The partial folds obtained from it
by identifying the two points of some of the pairs have 33397 +|S| vertices when a set S of pairs
is kept apart, and their degrees are determined by S and the colouring (Theorem 5.1). Blowing
up the points of the subhexagon H(3) (Theorem 5.2) and adding twins, we obtain the following
bounds.

2 Preliminaries
2.1 Generalized hexagons
All graphs are finite and simple, and N (x) denotes the set of neighbours of a vertex x. A
generalized hexagon of order (s, t) is a point-line incidence structure in which every line has s + 1
2

points, every point lies on t + 1 lines, and the incidence graph, the bipartite graph on points
and lines with edges given by incidence, has diameter 6 and girth 12 [19]. For order ( q, q) the
incidence graph is ( q + 1)-regular with 2(1 + q + q2 + q3 + q4 + q5) vertices. An automorphism
of the incidence graph either preserves the set of points, and is a collineation, or interchanges
points and lines, and is a duality. A polarity is a duality of order two, and a point x is absolute
for a polarity π if it lies on the line πx.
The points of the split Cayley hexagon H(q) are the points of the parabolic quadric Q(6, q)
of PG(6 , q) with equation X0X4 + X1X5 + X2X6 = X 2
3 , and its lines are the lines of Q(6, q)
whose Grassmann coordinates pij satisfy
p12 = p34, p 54 = p32, p 20 = p35, p 65 = p30, p 01 = p36, p 46 = p31.
It is a generalized hexagon of order ( q, q) [18, 19]. For q = 9 its incidence graph Γ has 132860
vertices. Raising every coordinate to its third power induces a collineation φ of H(9) of order
two. The points and lines it fixes are those defined over F3; they form a subhexagon H(3) with
364 points and 364 lines.
2.2 The fold of H(9)
The quotient Γ/G of a graph Γ by a group G of automorphisms has the G-orbits on V (Γ) as its
vertices, two distinct orbits being adjacent if some vertex of one is adjacent to some vertex of
the other.
Theorem 2.1([15, Theorem 1.1 and its proof]). H(9) has a duality τ of order four with τ 2 = φ,
and τ induces a polarity π of H(3) with 28 absolute points. The quotient F = Γ/⟨τ ⟩ has 33397
vertices and 166425 edges; 33033 of its vertices have degree 10, 336 have degree 7 and 28 have
degree 6; and F has diameter 5.
We fix such a duality τ , write φ = τ 2, and write P and L for the sets of points and lines
of H(9). Since τ commutes with φ, it maps the incidence graph of H(3) to itself; the polarity
π of Theorem 2.1 is its restriction, so τ h= πh for a point h of H(3), and as φh = h also
τ −1h = τ φh= τ h.
A point p outside H(3) lies in the fiber {p, φp}, of size two, and a point of H(3) forms a
fiber of its own. The orbit of p under ⟨τ ⟩ is {p, τ p, φp, τ−1p}, whose points are the two points
of the fiber of p, and the orbit of a point h of H(3) is {h, τ h}. Every orbit contains a point, so
the vertices of F correspond to the fibers, and we identify them. The 33033 fibers of size two
are the generic fibers; they are the vertices of degree 10 of F , and the 364 points of H(3) are
the others. We write ¯p for the fiber of a point p, and Q for the subgraph of F induced by the
generic fibers.
2.3 Twins
A twin of a vertex v is a new vertex adjacent to exactly the neighbours of v. Twins were used for
the degree-diameter problem by Delorme [6, 8] and by G´ omez, Fiol and Serra [11]; for example,
the largest known graphs of degree 10 and 11 and diameter 3 arise from the polarity graph of
W (8) by adding one and two twins of each of its 65 absolute points [10]. We use the following
form.

where k ≥ 3. For each vertex v choose an integer cv ≥ 0, and let H ′ be obtained from H by
adding cv twins of each vertex v, no two new vertices being adjacent. Then H ′ has diameter at
most k. A twin of v has degree degH v, and a vertex u of H has degree degH u + P
v∈N (u) cv in
H ′.
Proof. The degrees are clear. For a vertex z of H ′ let ρ(z) be the vertex of H of which z is a
twin, and let ρ(z) = z if z ∈ V (H). A new vertex z and a vertex u of H are adjacent if and only
if ρ(z) and u are adjacent. Let z, z′ be distinct vertices of H ′, and put a = ρ(z) and b = ρ(z′).
If a = b, then z and z′ are not adjacent and have the neighbours of a in common, so
d(z, z′) = 2. If dH (a, b) = t ≥ 2, let a, a1, . . . , at−1, bbe a path in H. Its inner vertices lie
in H, so z, a1, . . . , at−1, z′ is a path in H ′ and d(z, z′) ≤ t ≤ k. If a and b are adjacent and
one of z, z′ lies in H, then z and z′ are adjacent. If both are new, then z, b, a, z′ is a path, so
d(z, z′) ≤ 3 ≤ k.
3 Pairings of points with lines
Lemma 3.1. Let P and L be the points and lines of a generalized hexagon, and let σ : P → L
be a bijection. Let Gσ be the graph on P in which distinct points x and y are adjacent when y
lies on σ(x) or x lies on σ(y). Then Gσ has diameter at most 5.
Proof. Let x, y∈ P. The incidence graph is bipartite of diameter 6, so it has a path
x = x0, L1, x1, L2, . . . , xj−1, Lj = σ(y)
from x to the line σ(y) with 2 j − 1 ≤ 5. Put yi = σ−1(Li), so that yj = y. For 1 ≤ i ≤ j the
point xi−1 lies on σ(yi), and so does xi when i < j. Hence x0, y1, x1, . . . , xj−1, yj is a walk of
length 2j − 1 ≤ 5 in Gσ once repeated consecutive vertices are removed.
If σ is a polarity, then Gσ is the polarity graph, with its loops at the absolute points removed.
The lemma needs neither that σ is an automorphism nor any relation between σ(x) and σ(y):
any bijection will do. What σ controls is the degree. A point x is adjacent to the other points
of σ(x) and to the points σ−1(M ) for the lines M through x, so its degree is at most 2( q + 1) in
a hexagon of order ( q, q), and it is smaller when the two sets overlap. The pairings below make
the overlap as large as the fold allows.
4 Pairings from a duality of order four
Let ε : P → {0, 1} satisfy ε(φp) = ε(p) for every point p, and put σε(p) = τ pif ε(p) = 1 and
σε(p) = τ −1p if ε(p) = 0. For a point h of H(3) we have τ −1h = τ h, so the value of ε on H(3)
does not matter. We write Gε for Gσε.
Lemma 4.1. σε is a bijection from P to L, and φ is an automorphism of Gε. The map p 7→ ¯p
sends every edge of Gε to an edge of F , and no two points of one fiber are adjacent. Let ¯p and
¯r be adjacent in F .
(i) If both fibers are generic, the edges of Gε between them form a perfect matching when
ε(p) ̸= ε(r) and a complete bipartite graph K2,2 when ε(p) = ε(r).

(ii) If ¯p is generic and r is a point of H(3), then r is adjacent to both p and φp.
(iii) If p and r are points of H(3), they are adjacent.
Moreover, a point h of H(3) is adjacent in F to exactly three generic fibers, and to 3 or 4 points
of H(3) according as it is absolute for π or not, and a generic fiber is adjacent in F to at most
one point of H(3).
Proof. If σε(p) = σε(p′) with p ̸= p′, then ε(p) ̸= ε(p′), say τ p= τ −1p′, and then p′ = φp, which
has the colour of p. So σε is injective, hence bijective, as |P| = |L|. As φ commutes with τ and
preserves ε, it commutes with σε and is an automorphism of Gε.
For a point p let Rp be the set of the ten points of the line τ −1p. A line M lies on p if and
only if the point τ −1M lies on τ −1p, and the orbit of M contains the fiber of τ −1M . So the
neighbours of ¯p in F are the fibers of the points of Rp, other than ¯p itself.
Let p be a point outside H(3). The line τ −1p is not fixed by φ, since otherwise p = τ (τ −1p)
would be. So it contains no two points of one fiber, and at most one point of H(3), because two
such points would span a line fixed by φ. Nor does it contain p or φp. Since φ interchanges p
and φp and interchanges the lines τ pand φτ p= τ −1p, it suffices to show that neither p nor φp
lies on τ p. Applying τ to the incident pair ( p, τ p) gives the incident pair ( τ p, φp), and applying
τ −1 to (φp, τ p) gives (τ p, p), since τ −1φ = τ ; so p lies on τ pif and only if φp does. In that case

H(3). Hence the fibers of the points of Rp are ten distinct fibers different from ¯p, at most one
of which is a point of H(3); this is why generic fibers have degree 10 in F .

5 Partial folds
Choose a set S of generic fibers and a colouring c : S → {0, 1}. The partial fold G(S, c) is
obtained from Gε, with ε = c on the fibers in S and ε = 0 elsewhere, by identifying the two
points of every generic fiber outside S. We call the fibers in S split and the other generic fibers
merged. For a generic fiber f let h(f ) be its neighbour in H(3), if it has one; by Lemma 4.1,
3 · 364 = 1092 generic fibers have one.
Theorem 5.1. G(S, c) has 33397 + |S| vertices and diameter at most 5. A merged fiber f has
degree 10 +|NQ(f ) ∩ S|; a point of a fiber f ∈ S has degree 10 +|{g ∈ NQ(f ) ∩ S : c(g) = c(f )}|;
5

and a point h of H(3) has degree 7 − [h absolute] + s(h), where s(h) is the number of fibers in
S among the three generic fibers adjacent to h.
Proof. There are 364 + 2|S| + (33033 − |S|) vertices. By Lemma 4.1 the two points of a merged
fiber are not adjacent, so the identification creates no loop, and identifying two vertices does
not increase any distance; so the diameter bound follows from Lemma 3.1. The degrees follow
from Lemma 4.1: a merged fiber meets a merged neighbour or a point of H(3) in one edge and
a split neighbour in two; a point of a split fiber meets a merged neighbour or a point of H(3)
once, and a split neighbour once or twice according as its colour differs or agrees; and a point
h of H(3) meets each of its 4 − [h absolute] neighbours in H(3) once and each adjacent generic
fiber once, or twice if the fiber is split.
For S = ∅ the partial fold is F itself, and for S the set of all generic fibers and a constant
colouring it is Gε itself, in which a point outside H(3) has degree 20, or 19 if its fiber is adjacent
to a point of H(3). The theorem shows that splitting a fiber costs one unit of degree at each
merged neighbour, and at each split neighbour of the same colour, so the colouring decides where
the extra degree goes.
Finally, we blow up the points of H(3). For cH : H(3) → Z≥0, replace each point h of H(3)
by 1 +cH (h) pairwise non-adjacent copies, each adjacent to every copy of every neighbour of h,
and call the result G(S, c, cH ).

Proof. Copies of distinct vertices x and y are at distance d(x, y): a path lifts to copies, and the

and 5.2 stay within δ at every vertex, a system of linear inequalities in the indicator variables ofS

6 Verification and data
The dataset, deposited at Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.23056128), contains the duality τ as a
permutation of the 132860 vertices of Γ and, for each graph of Table 2, a witness file, which
gives the set S, the colouring c, the counts cH and the vertices that the further twins copy,
and an explicit edge list. A standalone script in Python, which uses only the standard library
and shares no code with the search programs, checks the SHA-256 hash of each edge list, that
the graph is simple with maximum degree d, and that the eccentricity is 5 at a fixed sample of
vertices, or at every vertex on request. On request it also rebuilds Γ from the coordinates of
Section 2, checks that τ is a duality of order four whose square fixes 728 vertices, and rebuilds
every graph from its witness, which reproduces the shipped edge list edge for edge. Breadth-first
search from every vertex, by two further programs, one of them in C and included in the dataset,
found every eccentricity equal to 5; these graphs are not vertex-transitive, so a search from one
vertex would not suffice. The graphs of Appendix B are listed but not included.
